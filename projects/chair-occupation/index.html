Vision Deep Learning YOLOv8

Chair occupancy detection on a deliberately small dataset

Detecting whether a specific chair is free, in real time, from a handful of training images — and choosing the architecture on evidence rather than on version number.

The goal

A room-level occupancy sensor that needs no sensor in the furniture. Point a camera at a space and have it report which chairs are free — eventually running entirely on an Arduino UNO Q rather than on a workstation.

The current model detects the unoccupied chair reliably. The harder class — distinguishing a chair with someone in it, where the object of interest is largely occluded by the very thing that makes it interesting — is still in progress, and I'd rather say so than round it up.

Choosing the architecture

The obvious move was to reach for the newest YOLO release. I trained both and compared them instead, and the newer version lost.

ModelF1 peak confidenceBounding boxesChosen
YOLOv80.644Tighter, more consistentYes
YOLOv110.357Looser on the target objectNo

The F1–confidence curve is the useful artefact here. YOLOv8 peaks at a high confidence threshold, which means the model is genuinely certain about its correct detections. YOLOv11 peaking at 0.357 means it only achieves its best balance of precision and recall by accepting predictions it isn't confident in — a much more fragile place to set an operating point when the lighting changes.

The threshold is a design decision, not a default. Reading it off the F1 curve rather than leaving it at 0.25 is the difference between a demo and something you'd actually deploy.

On that 99.5%

It's a real number, and it's also a small dataset of one specific chair. It says the training pipeline and the labels are sound; it does not say the model would survive a different room. Treating a headline metric as a claim about the world rather than about the test set is the fastest way to be wrong in public, so: this is a well-fit detector on a narrow problem, and the next step is widening the problem.
