Control allocation when a rotor loses effectiveness

PX4's allocator assumes every rotor delivers what it is told to. I added a per-rotor effectiveness scale, injected partial failures in simulation, and compared two ways of redistributing thrust. Hover survives moderate loss; the interesting behaviour is at the edge.

1The question

A quadrotor has four actuators for a four-dimensional command (thrust plus three torques). Map a desired wrench to motor commands through the effectiveness matrix B:

u = B+ τ(1)

with B+ its pseudo-inverse.With four actuators and a four-dimensional command, B is square. Scaling one rotor's column down shrinks the authority available in that direction, and at 0% the matrix loses rank. Once commands saturate, the allocator has to give something up, and which thing is the experiment. When one rotor degrades, the command no longer maps to the force that rotor actually produces. What does the allocator do, and does the vehicle stay in the air?

2What I changed

  • A per-rotor effectiveness scale inside ActuatorEffectivenessRotors, scaling each column of B.
  • A failure_scheduler module that steps a chosen rotor through a sequence of effectiveness levels on a timer.
  • A RotorEffectiveness uORB message so the scale is visible to the rest of the stack and to logging.

3Reference: no change to the allocator

The baseline is a hard motor failure with the stock allocator. The allocator responded about 504 ms after injection and the vehicle then lost attitude.

4Sweeping effectiveness

The sweep steps one rotor from 100% to 75%, 50%, 25% and 0% effectiveness at a hold altitude of 20 m. With sequential desaturation the vehicle held hover at 75% and 50%, each with a transient peaking near 0.85 command that damped out in about 1.5 s.

The comparison that matters is how thrust is redistributed once a rotor can no longer deliver its share:

Motor commands at 50% effectiveness on one rotor (normalised). The two strategies are close; the difference is in how the shortfall is shared.

The two runs differ at 25%: the recorded sequential-desaturation run lost altitude, the pseudo-inverse run did not. I have not yet repeated either run, so I would not call that a ranking of the two methods.

5Limits

  • Each configuration was run once, so I have no measure of run-to-run variation.
  • The 0% case was not measured at altitude in the recorded sweep.
  • Simulation only: no motor dynamics beyond what Gazebo models, no real ESC or battery sag.

6Next

Repeat each configuration across seeds, then compare the two allocators on the 25% case with error bars. A learned allocation policy is the obvious follow-up, and the place where I would want a stability argument to sit alongside the reward curve.
